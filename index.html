Abstract

        Engine efficiency is of high importance in today’s world as society moves toward reducing
        greenhouse gas emissions and helping consumers reduce costs. While there are many variables
        that affect the fuel efficiency of cars, in this article we will specifically be looking at
        the role of superchargers and turbochargers.

Introduction

        In petrol engines, a component known as the crankshaft is joined to each piston via a
        connecting rod to convert the pistons’ oscillating motion into rotary motion. The pistons
        lie within combustion chambers where air from the intake and fuel from the tank are injected
        in a controlled quantity. As NASA explains, “During the intake stroke, the piston moves down
        and draws in a mixture of fuel and air. The piston then moves up, compressing the mixture, 
        and near the top of the stroke the spark plug ignites the mixture, causing combustion and
        forcing the piston down” (NASA Glenn Research Center, 2023). This downward force is what
        generates torque (also known as a moment) in the crankshaft, which is then transferred to the
        wheels through the transmission. The pistons are timed such that as one piston moves down,
        another moves up, allowing the cycle to repeat smoothly. The basic principle of supercharging
        or turbocharging a vehicle is to increase the rate of airflow through the intake, as more air
        leads to more efficient combustion. To achieve this, both superchargers and turbochargers
        compress the incoming air using different methods.

Turbochargers

        Turbochargers use the exhaust fumes that are expelled from each piston stroke to increase the
        air intake. To do this, a turbine is connected to the exhaust and spools up as the engine gets
        rid of the waste gasses from combustion (sometimes reaching speeds up to 200,000 Rotations per
        minute in high performance cars). This is explained in the book Turbocharging the Internal Combustion
        Engine. "Turbochargers utilize the energy from exhaust gases to spin a turbine connected to a
        compressor, which increases the pressure and density of the intake air, resulting in more efficient
        combustion" (Watson and Janota, 1982). Although compressing the air will lead to more efficien
        combustion because the air has been compressed, the temperature of the air has also increased
        as pressure and temperature are directly proportional. To reverse this, before the air reaches
        the combustion chamber, it must first cool down through an intercooler. This combination of
        processes leads to cold, dense air, ideal for efficient combustion. 
        
        However, while turbochargers come with the benefit of fuel economy and better performance, they
        come at the cost of time. Turbochargers are dependent on the exhaust flow of the engine, before
        boost can be produced the engine must first build up enough exhaust pressure to spool up the
        turbine. This leads to a slight delay which can range between a fraction of a second, to over
        a second. Some variables that affect the duration of this lag include the size/weight of the
        turbocharger (affects the rotational inertia of the turbine), the volume of the compression
        chamber (affects the volume of exhaust gases being expelled per piston stroke), and the RPM of
        the engine (affects the rate at which exhaust gasses are expelled). To summarise, the main
        benefit of Turbochargers is that they use waste kinetic energy from the exhaust and repurposes it,
        saving fuel and improving power. This is why turbochargers are found in many modern cars as well
        as performance cars.

Superchargers

        Superchargers use a belt connected directly to the crankshaft to compress the air. You could see this
        as the engine ‘stealing’ power from itself to itself to improve combustion. Superchargers share
        the same cooling process as turbochargers through an intercooler. The big difference in superchargers
        is that the boost is instant, unlike turbochargers. This is because the belt is connected directly
        to the crankshaft, as soon as the engine is running, air is compressed, no need to wait for anything
        to build up. This means that at lower engine RPM’s (the number of crankshaft rotations per minute
        i.e. the engines speed) the car is still able to compress air. This is beneficial for performance cars
        as they will accelerate quicker at lower speeds. 
        
        It appears superchargers solve the problem of turbo lag, however, unfortunately this comes at a cost
        as well. Because the engine is ‘stealing’ power from the engine, this also requires more fuel, leading
        to higher emissions and lower fuel efficiency.
